# Fermentation Feedstock Economics

What a precision-fermentation host organism is fed is the largest lever on the cost of the
finished product, and the substrate a company chooses is the single fact that predicts most
of the rest of its cost, risk, and funding story.

## The cost breakdown

Feedstock (media) is reported as 35-50% of cost of goods sold for a typical precision
fermentation process, ahead of facility depreciation (20-25%) and downstream processing
(15-20%) - though for some biologic products, extraction and purification alone can run up to
85% of cost, which can outweigh feedstock choice entirely
([[Precision Fermentation Pathways to Cost Parity (insight)]]). Both figures matter: a cheap
substrate does not guarantee a cheap product if the target molecule is hard to purify from the
broth.

## Substrate categories, cheapest-to-riskiest as currently funded

- **Refined sugar/glucose (corn, wheat, sugarcane)** - the incumbent substrate; most funded
  companies still use it. Cheap and well-understood, but the bioeconomy's total glucose
  demand is projected to outgrow supply, and it competes with food and biofuel uses for the
  same crops ([[Most Sustainable Feedstocks for Precision Fermentation (article)]]).
- **Dairy and food-industry side-streams** - e.g. [[Standing Ovation]] feeds its host on acid
  whey left over from cottage cheese and Greek yogurt production, supplemented with sugar; the
  company frames this explicitly as avoiding "buy[ing] food to build food." Hyfe processes
  brewery and cannery wastewater into standardized microbial feed, pitched as cutting cost and
  environmental footprint simultaneously.
- **Lignocellulosic (forestry/agricultural) waste** - hardwood residues broken into C5/C6
  sugars; requires a hydrolysis pretreatment step that adds cost and complexity. Fibenol
  (Estonia) runs a 20,000-tonne/year plant supplying this substrate to fermentation users such
  as ÄIO.
- **Methanol and other C1 feedstocks** - used with methylotrophic hosts such as *Pichia
  pastoris*; liquid at room temperature, which makes it easier to handle than a gas feedstock.
  [[Fermeate]]'s optogenetic control system is pitched partly as a way to swap a chemical
  inducer like methanol for light control, changing the substrate-handling cost rather than
  the substrate itself.
- **CO2 / hydrogen / methane (gas fermentation)** - the highest-upside, highest-risk category.
  [[Solar Foods]] feeds its bacteria CO2 and water via electrolytic hydrogen; Calysta and
  LanzaTech feed theirs waste methane and industrial off-gases; [[Arkeon]] used CO2 and
  hydrogen (initially grey, i.e. natural-gas-derived, hydrogen, for cost reasons) before
  filing for insolvency in 2026. Investors reportedly treat gas fermentation as "definitely
  higher" risk than sugar- or side-stream-fed processes even when the unit-economics case
  pencils out on paper ([[Arkeon Postmortem (article)]]).

## In practice

- When comparing two funding rounds, check the feedstock before comparing the dollar amount:
  a sugar-fed dairy-protein round and a CO2-fed gas-fermentation round are different risk
  categories dressed in the same headline term.
- A claim of "unit economics improving" is a claim about feedstock price, downstream yield, or
  both - ask which, since the two move independently.
- Treat a "protein from air" (CO2/gas) pitch as pre-commercial-risk even after a large seed or
  Series A: [[Arkeon]] raised over $13M before insolvency, and closing a round is not the same
  signal of substrate viability that it is for a sugar- or whey-fed process.
